Guidance for teachers

How to run Guess It Maths in a lesson, and how to support or stretch different groups.

Running a round

The game works like Guess Who, adapted for a whole class.

  1. Choose a topic and put the board on the interactive whiteboard.
  2. Invite a student to the front to choose a card and show it only to you. Alternatively, press Secret card and let the game pick one at random. You can press and hold to peek at it privately.
  3. Students think of a yes/no question. Take hands up, or have everyone write a question on a mini whiteboard and pick students at random.
  4. Answer the question and tap every card it rules out. Tap a card again to turn it back up, or use Undo if you make a mistake.
  5. When one card is left, the game reveals the secret card and tells you whether the class got it right.

Use the + button to count questions as you go. Fewer questions to reach the answer means a better round, which makes a good target for the class to beat.

Supporting and stretching

Every board has a panel of question ideas beside the cards. The Warm-up questions help students who need a starting point. The Stretch questions push the strongest students to use more precise vocabulary and deeper properties.

To make the game harder, add a rule about the questions allowed. The yellow challenge box suggests these, for example asking a question without naming a shape, or one that rules out at least four cards.

To make it easier, turn on the Names, Hints or Decimals button (depending on the topic). Printing a keyword list also helps students who struggle to phrase questions.

History of maths

The History of Maths topic will sit alongside a few bonus topics on pi, the golden ratio and fractals, giving an in-depth look at some deeper ideas in mathematics. These work well for strong students who want to learn more, or for filling form time and end-of-term lessons.

What’s next

Once every topic is finished, we plan to create easy, medium and hard versions of each one, either through different prompt sheets or separate boards, so the game works across more year groups. After that, we’re looking at:

  • New topics, including trigonometry
  • A single-player version where you play against the computer
  • An improved whole-class version

We’re also working on another online game that combines maths with a classic childhood board game. We hope it will make a splash (pun intended).
